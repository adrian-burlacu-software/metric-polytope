# Is there a short generative description of the vertices of the metric polytope?

*Computational study, n ≤ 9. Report of 2026-11-25. Details, scripts and round-by-round history: `docs/phase9_status.md`.*

## Summary

The metric polytope m_n has 13, 533 and at least 1,056,367 orbits of vertices for n = 7, 8, 9. We asked whether
these can be produced by a fixed set of rules valid for every n, plus a list of exceptions, instead of by vertex
enumeration. Through n = 9 the answer is no in every form we tried, and each failure is measured:

- Rules that build a vertex from a smaller vertex cannot produce more than 36.2% of the m_9 orbits, whatever the rules
  are, because 63.8% of the orbits contain no smaller vertex.
- Without that restriction a uniform normal form does exist: every known vertex of m_9 is built from at most 5 nodes
  by one-node steps of six kinds. But the kinds do not determine which step to take, and a generator built from them
  is a search whose size grows like the 9th to 11th power of the denominator.
- Families indexed by a graph need more and more weight patterns (2, 9, 1,273 for n = 7, 8, 9), and a graph does not
  determine its weights: at n = 9 one graph carries 34 vertices on average and up to 2,177.

The positive results are the normal form, the exact ceiling on restriction-keeping rules, and a measurement of where
the difficulty sits: the part of the search that leads to vertices is about as large as the answer, and the cost is
the choice of that part.

## 1. Setting

m_n is the set of x in R^C(n,2) with x_ij ≤ x_ik + x_jk and x_ij + x_ik + x_jk ≤ 2 for all triples. Its symmetry
group is generated by the permutations of the n nodes and the switchings (x_ij ↦ 1 − x_ij on the edges of a cut);
"orbit" always means an orbit of this group. The cuts are the integral vertices and form one orbit. The denominator D
of a vertex is the least common denominator of its coordinates.

Data: the complete vertex lists for n ≤ 8 (533 orbits at n = 8, completeness re-proved in this project) and Deza's
list of 1,056,367 orbits for n = 9, which its authors call probably complete. Everything below is exact (integer
arithmetic; ranks modulo 2^31 − 1 with an exact fallback whenever the modular rank is deficient).

## 2. Rules that build vertices from smaller vertices

A *move* takes a vertex of m_{n−1} and adds a node, keeping the input as the restriction to the old nodes. In 17
rounds of proposing and testing such rules, 8 moves and 5 base families were accepted. Together they reach 511 of the
533 orbits of m_8 and 513 of a fixed set of 1,000 orbits of m_9.

Two measurements show that this is a limit of the rule type, not of the particular rules.

**Primitive vertices.** Call a vertex primitive if none of its restrictions to 3 ≤ k < n nodes is a vertex of m_k. No
move can produce a primitive vertex.

| n | orbits | primitive | share |
|---|---|---|---|
| 7 | 13 | 4 | 31% |
| 8 | 533 | 219 | 41% |
| 9 | 1,056,367 | 673,787 | 63.8% |

At n = 9, 159,525 orbits have a vertex restriction on 8 nodes, 7,316 on 7 but not 8, 500 on 6 at most, and 215,239
on 5 at most.

**Capacity of the accepted moves.** All 8 moves applied to all 533 orbits of m_8 give 70,047 outputs that are
vertices of m_9, hence at most 6.6% of the orbits (131 per input on average, at most 1,456). The number of orbits
grows by a factor 41 and then 1,982 per node; a move with a bounded choice gives polynomially many outputs per input.

Base families (explicit constructions of primitive vertices) reached the remaining orbits at one n and did not
continue to n + 1.

## 3. A normal form: one-node steps over a small seed

Drop the requirement that intermediate points are vertices. Say node v can be *peeled* from the node set S of a
vertex x if the coordinates x_va, a ∈ S − v, are determined by the tight triangle inequalities inside S that contain
v (x|S is a vertex of the one-node fiber over x|(S − v)). Peel until no node can be peeled; the remaining set is a
seed.

**Seeds are small.** Over the whole m_9 list: seed of 3 nodes for 583,636 orbits, 4 nodes for 472,722, 5 nodes for 8
(denominators 3, 3, 3, 9, 9, 9, 11, 12). Every orbit of m_8 has a seed of at most 4 nodes.

**Steps come in six kinds.** A tight triangle (v, a, b) gives an equation x_va − x_vb = c or x_va + x_vb = c. These
form a signed graph on S − v, and the step is determined exactly when every connected component contains an
unbalanced cycle (odd number of sum equations); the rest of the component propagates values along a tree. A step is
therefore described by its anchors, the shortest unbalanced cycle of each component. For each vertex take the chain
that reaches the smallest seed and then has the shortest longest anchor. Over the 1,056,366 non-cut orbits of m_9:

| step kind (anchor lengths) | steps |
|---|---|
| one 3-cycle | 5,760,177 |
| two components, 3-cycles | 88,230 |
| one 5-cycle | 11,967 |
| one 2-cycle (a sum and a difference on the same pair) | 3,497 |
| one 7-cycle | 1,565 |
| two components, a 3-cycle and a 5-cycle | 22 |

98.7% of the orbits need 3-cycles only. The first five kinds already occur at n = 8; the sixth combines two of them.
A family with unbounded denominators (Avis's doubling of a path, n = 8, 11, 14, denominators 4, 5, 6) needs 3-cycles
only, over all chains. The chain leaves the seed's coordinates free; they are fixed at the end by the remaining tight
inequalities, and that is where the denominators arise.

## 4. The step kinds do not make a shortcut

**A complete generator.** For a target denominator D: start from all points of m_3 and m_4 with coordinates in
(1/D)Z, extend by every determined step that stays in (1/D)Z, merge orbits, and test the 8-node points for being
vertices. On m_8 it finds every orbit (52, 12, 15, 95 for D = 3, 4, 5, 6) and nothing else, but:

| D | orbits at 7 nodes | candidates at 8 nodes | vertices among them |
|---|---|---|---|
| 3 | 64 | 2,958 | 43% |
| 4 | 670 | 67,164 | 0.28% |
| 5 | 4,446 | 757,052 | 0.04% |
| 6 | 22,788 | 5,215,846 | 0.07% |

The growth from D = 3 to 6 corresponds to D^9 to D^11; m_8 has denominators up to 15.

**The useful part is small.** Call a partial point useful if it is the restriction of a vertex along a valid chain.
At D = 6 the useful orbits number 39, 92, 200, 287 at 4 to 7 nodes (the generator has 85, 311, 1,871, 22,788), ending
in 147 vertex orbits. A useful point has 2 to 5 useful extensions among 4 to 65.

**No local feature picks them.** Six features of an extension were tested as a safe filter (one that never discards
a useful extension): the number of tight inequalities beyond the dimension, the number through the new node, whether
the extension is itself a vertex, its anchor, the denominator of the new coordinates, and whether the new node
duplicates an old one. At the intermediate steps the best of them keeps 86–100% of the options while 17–56% are
useful. Useful intermediates are, if anything, less degenerate than the worst useless ones.

## 5. Families indexed by a graph

Every vertex of m_8, and all but 1,221 orbits of m_9, is adjacent to a cut (the 1,221 match the published count for
the failure of the Laurent–Poljak conjecture at n = 9). Such a vertex is, after a switching, a scaled extreme ray of
the metric cone, hence the shortest-path metric of a weighted graph: keep the pairs with no node exactly between
them, with coprime integer weights.

| n | vertices | sets of weights that occur | unweighted graphs (all weights 1) | distinct underlying graphs | most vertices on one graph |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 5, 6 | 1, 2 | 1 | 100% | 1, 2 | 1 |
| 7 | 12 | 2 | 92% | 11 | 1 |
| 8 | 532 | 9 | 20% | 256 | 12 |
| 9 | 1,055,145 | 1,273 | 0.52% | 31,061 | 2,177 |

89.5% of the m_9 orbits use an interval of weights {a, ..., b} (48 intervals; the largest weight is 21), so the
weight sets themselves are simple. But the family that is all of m_5 and m_6 is 0.52% of m_9, and the 1,055,145
orbits sit on only 31,061 graphs: 34 per graph on average, 2,177 on one, and only 5,910 graphs carry a single
vertex. A rule "graph ↦ vertex" needs the weights as additional input.

**The simplest weighted family.** For the rays with weights 1 and 2 only (1, 3, 28, 925 on 5 to 8 nodes; 27,963
known on 9), a deduction rule on geodesics (opposite sides of a geodesic rectangle are equal; odd chains of such
exchanges; then triangles with one unknown) is a proved sufficient condition and is also necessary through 8 nodes.
At 9 nodes it misses 76 of the 27,963 rays, and 46 of 46,436 further rays built on 9 to 11 nodes. The best necessary
condition found rejects 92% of near-miss non-rays. No exact combinatorial characterisation was found
(`docs/phase9_status.md` §18).

**Two results that close the question for bounded rules** (`docs/phase9_status.md` §20, §21).

*Counting.* By a theorem of Avis (1980, Thm 3.3) almost all graphs on n nodes induce extreme rays, so m_n has at least
2^(n²/2 − O(n log n)) orbits of vertices. A generator made of finitely many rules, each choosing among at most
2^((1−δ)n) alternatives when it adds the n-th node, produces at most 2^((1−δ)n²/2 + O(n)) objects. Its share of the
orbits tends to 0, so it has infinitely many exceptions. Every rule type of §2–§4 is of this kind.

*Locality.* For rules that take a whole weighted graph as input, measure how many nodes must be examined together to
prove the ray (solve the tight equalities inside every s-subset, merge, repeat). The smallest sufficient s is 4 for
all of m_7; at n = 8 it is 4 for 76% of the orbits and reaches 7; at n = 9 (30,008 sampled) it is 4 for 29%, 5 for
48%, 6 for 20%, and reaches 8. Rules with a fixed inspection size lose ground at every n.

## 6. Conclusions

1. Restriction-keeping rules cannot describe m_9: at least 63.8% of its orbits are out of their reach.
2. Every known vertex of m_9 has the normal form of §3. This is a uniform structural statement about more than a
   million orbits, and the 8 orbits with a 5-node seed are its only irregularity.
3. The normal form does not give a construction. The cost of generating m_n is the cost of choosing, among the
   determined steps, the few that lead to vertices; within the features tested this choice is not local.
4. Graph-indexed families do not stay finite in number either.
5. "Finitely many rules plus finitely many exceptions" is impossible for rules with a small choice per node
   (proved), and for graph-input rules the number of points a rule must inspect is unbounded (proved: an explicit
   family of extreme rays on 2t points needs more than t/6 points, and all 2t in the cases computed; see
   `docs/paper_draft.md`, Theorem 4).

## 7. Limits

- n ≤ 9. For n = 10 there are only 7 sampled vertices (random walks on the skeleton); their steps are all 3-cycles.
- The m_9 list is not proved complete. Statements about "all of m_9" are about the known list.
- The generator measurements are for m_8 and D ≤ 6; larger D is extrapolated.
- "No local feature" refers to the six features of §4.
- The graph statistics at n = 9 use one switching per orbit (the one with the simplest weight set).
- A side test of the distance to the elliptope (smallest eigenvalue of J − 2X) found no relation to which orbits the
  rules reach.
